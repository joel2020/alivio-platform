## Draft 1: choosing a recruiting engagement

Suggested destination: a concise FAQ section on `/services`.

### Which recruiting engagement fits your hiring need?

Start with the hiring problem. A single leadership appointment, a recurring need for the same skill set, and a temporary recruiting-capacity gap call for different scopes. Alivio describes three engagement models: retained search, pipeline programs, and project or RPO support. Use the initial conversation to agree which work is included, who makes decisions, and how progress will be reported.

**When should we discuss retained search?**

When the organization has a defined, consequential role to fill and needs a dedicated search process. Bring the role's expected outcomes, reporting relationships, decision-makers and compensation parameters. Agree the scope and commercial terms in writing before work begins.

**When is an ongoing pipeline useful?**

When a team repeatedly hires similar roles or needs sustained market coverage. A useful discussion starts with hiring frequency, locations, skills and the internal team's capacity to interview and decide. Agree what happens when demand changes.

**What should project or RPO support include?**

Define the work the external team will own, the systems it will use, the reporting cadence and the handover. Separate sourcing, screening, coordination and decision authority so responsibilities are clear.

**What should we expect in a shortlist?**

Ask for a consistent role scorecard, evidence relevant to the role, the candidate's stated availability, unanswered questions and a reasoned explanation of fit. A shortlist is a decision aid; it is not proof that every important question has been resolved.

**What determines fees and timing?**

Role scope, location, market constraints, assessment requirements and the agreed engagement all matter. Request a written proposal rather than assuming a universal fee or timeline. No new price, replacement guarantee or turnaround promise is supplied in this draft.

Owner checks: verify engagement definitions, deliverables, reporting commitments, commercial process and whether contingent search belongs in this comparison.

## Draft 2: a physician search kickoff checklist

Suggested destination: one substantial guide linked from `/industries/healthcare`, after recruiter review.

### Before you open a physician search, align the people making the decision

A useful search brief does more than name a specialty. It gives everyone involved the same understanding of the role and the decisions still to be made. Use this checklist to prepare the kickoff conversation:

- **Role and care setting:** specialty, clinical responsibilities, sites, schedule and call expectations.
- **Decision process:** the people responsible for screening, interviews, compensation approval and the final offer.
- **Requirements:** the qualifications the organization must verify, and which preferences are negotiable. Have the appropriate credentialing team confirm requirements.
- **Candidate proposition:** what the organization can accurately explain about the work, team, location, support and development opportunities.
- **Compensation conversation:** approved range or framework, the person authorized to discuss it, and unresolved questions.
- **Evidence and evaluation:** the questions every interviewer will address and the evidence used to assess fit.
- **Timing:** the desired start, internal dependencies and how delays will be escalated.
- **Candidate communication:** who gives updates and what happens after each interview.

Document assumptions separately from verified facts. Revisit the brief when candidate feedback shows a mismatch between the role and the market. Credentialing, compensation and contractual decisions remain with the organization's qualified advisers and responsible staff.

## Draft 3: an engineering shortlist scorecard

Suggested destination: `/industries/technology` resource linked to `/product`.

### Compare engineering candidates against the work they will do

Use the same questions across candidates while making space for different kinds of experience. Start with the outcomes expected in the role, then decide what evidence would support confidence in those outcomes.

| Area | Evidence to discuss | Open question |
|---|---|---|
| Relevant delivery | A specific system or feature the candidate helped deliver | What did they personally own? |
| Technical decisions | A trade-off involving reliability, maintainability or delivery time | What alternatives did they consider? |
| Collaboration | An example of working with product, design or operations | How were disagreements resolved? |
| Context | Experience relevant to the company's stage and constraints | What would be new in this environment? |
| Role alignment | Candidate-stated interests, availability and working preferences | Which expectations still need agreement? |

Record evidence and uncertainty rather than treating a single numerical score as a conclusion. Use role-relevant questions consistently and keep protected or irrelevant personal information out of the scorecard. AI-assisted summaries should point reviewers back to the underlying evidence; a recruiter or hiring manager remains responsible for evaluating it.
